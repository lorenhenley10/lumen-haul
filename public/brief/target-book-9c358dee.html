01 — The model

Five marks of a client worth chasing

Hold these five in your head and you can size up a company in about a minute. Three is a good lead, four is a strong one, and mark 01 is close to mandatory — we point cameras at real things.

01

There is a physical thing to shoot

A car, a wheel, a machine, a product, a stage full of people. Software with nothing to point a lens at is a harder job and a smaller one.

Could I describe what the camera is looking at?

02

They will need it again

A product line, a build calendar, an event series, a roster of customers. One thing to shoot once is a single invoice; twelve is a year.

What do they launch, build or run on a schedule?

03

Someone there owns a budget

A named person with a marketing, brand, events or talent budget. At small shops that is the owner; at bigger ones it is rarely the founder and never the intern.

Can I name the person and their title?

04

They are close, or they will pay travel

San Francisco and San Jose carry no travel charge. Anywhere else is billed on top — fine, as long as they know before the estimate goes out.

Inside 90 minutes? If not, will travel be paid?

05

Their current content is worse than ours

Look at their Instagram and their website hero. A company posting mediocre work cares enough to publish and not enough to be good at it. That gap is the pitch.

Is our reel visibly better than what they have now?

—

And one thing that is not a mark

Company size. A twelve-person wheel brand with a launch calendar beats a 400-person company that shoots one video a year. Do not filter by headcount or funding stage — the budget question sorts that out.

What a job is actually worth, today. Nearly everything we close is a $500 to $3,200 full project. That is the real centre of gravity — a $1,200 job is a normal good day, not a disappointment. Above it sits a $3,200 to $8,000 tier: multi-day work, several deliverables, and usually a second shooter or an assistant hired in for it. Past $8,000 is rare enough to be a phone call, and the honest ceiling right now is a $20,000 automotive commercial — a real possibility, not a forecast. You are never quoting these — they are here so you can tell a promising answer from a weak one.
Where we actually are. Lumen Haul is early. The reel is genuinely good and the client names on it are real, but it is short, and we cannot yet win a job on reputation alone. That is why the ranges here sit below what an established studio would quote for the same work — at this stage we are buying the portfolio as well as the invoice. Price accordingly. Do not apologise for the number, and do not inflate it and lose the job.
Every name in this brief is clickable. Click to mark it contacted, then replied, then handed over. A fourth click drops it off the list — use that when they say no, and Declined in the bar brings them back if you need one. It saves in your browser, so it is there tomorrow and nobody else sees it. “You have a product that only sells when it looks like the reason someone bought the car. We shoot it that way, on a schedule, for a set number per vehicle.”

This buyer is not shopping for art direction. They want a repeatable job at a known price — their calendar runs on product launches and event dates. Proof to lead with: Blaque Diamond Wheels, Formula Drift, Shoreline Motoring, Hotpit Autofest.

05 — Where to find them

The lists to work from

All of these are free or close to it, and each produces a structured list rather than guesswork. Work them in order. Aim for around 40 good names a week, not 400 scraped ones.

Automotive
  1. SEMA Show exhibitor directory The best single list in the industry — roughly 2,400 companies, searchable and filterable by product category. Pull wheels, suspension and exhaust into a sheet and that alone is a quarter of work.
  2. SEMA New Products Showcase Same show, sharper filter. A brand entering a new product has a launch budget allocated right now.
  3. Distributor brand portfolios Turn 14, Keystone and Wheel Pros each list hundreds of brands publicly. Every one has a marketing function.
  4. The Instagram tag method Find the best fifteen automotive photographers in the country and look at who they tag. Those companies already pay for this work — the qualifying is done for you.
  5. Bring a Trailer and Cars & Bids seller pages Dealers listing repeatedly at high value. Their listing quality tells you instantly whether they need us.
  6. Hemmings dealer directory Classic and collector dealers by state. Start with California.
  7. PRI directory The motorsport equivalent of SEMA, with a harder-core parts buyer.
  8. Event vendor lists Wekfest, Import Alliance, Monterey Car Week. Everyone listed paid to be there, which means they spend on marketing.
Bay Area tech
  1. VC portfolio pages Start here, before Crunchbase. a16z, Sequoia, Lightspeed and Greylock publish every portfolio company on a free, structured page.
  2. Crunchbase, filtered Bay Area, funded in the last six months. Include seed — just ask about budget early rather than assuming there is none.
  3. The hiring cross-reference The single best move in this document. Take the funded list, then search LinkedIn for people who started as Head of Brand, Content Lead or Creative Director there in the last 90 days. That overlap is the target list.
  4. Stale job postings Search LinkedIn Jobs for “Video Producer” and “Content Producer” in the Bay Area. Anything open past 60 days is budget with nobody to spend it. Email the hiring manager, not HR.
  5. Conference sponsor lists A sponsor list is a list of companies with budget, already qualified. Pull Dreamforce, GTC, SaaStr and the developer conferences.
  6. Y Combinator directory Everything past Series A first, then a lighter pass over the current batch.
  7. Customer logo walls Open a big software company’s customers page. Every logo is a potential customer story, and the software company often pays for the film.
  8. Hardware newsletters The Robot Report, IEEE Spectrum, hardware funding trackers. Physical-product companies fit us best and are hardest to find by keyword.

The same email to the wrong person fails. In tech, aiming too high gets it forwarded down and lost. In automotive, aiming too low gets nothing at all, because below the owner nobody controls spend.

Three rules for every email. One: reference something specific and recent — a product, a round, a booth, a build. The specificity is the credibility, and generic emails get deleted. Two: lead with the frame, not a reel link; the reel goes in the second email, once they have replied. Three: keep it under 120 words and end with one ask, not two.
07 — The budget question

This is the thing you are actually being paid to find out

Everything before this section exists to get you into a conversation. This is the conversation. You are not quoting, negotiating or scoping — you are finding out what number they have in mind, then handing them to Loren.

“Have you had something like this made before? Roughly what did that run?”

The best opener by a distance. It asks about the past rather than about their limit, so it does not feel like being priced — and people answer it honestly.

“Is there a number this needs to land under, so I point Loren at the right scope?”

Frames the question as helping them rather than qualifying them. The phrase “the right scope” does a lot of work.

“Is this coming out of a set marketing budget, or approved case by case?”

Use it when they dodge the number. The answer tells you almost as much — a set budget means real money and a client who can repeat.

“What did the booth itself cost, out of interest?”

For events and shows. People who will not name a video budget will happily tell you what the booth cost, and it scales predictably from there.

What their answer means

Under $500

Below the floor for a day on site. Close it out politely — no counter-offer, no discount, no “let me check.” This is the one number you can decide on by yourself.

Skip
“No idea”

Not a dead lead. Ask what they spent on their last launch, their booth, or their website. If there is genuinely no money anywhere, park it and note a date to try again.

Dig once
$500–$1,500

A normal job, and the answer you will hear most often. Hand it over without hesitating — this is the bread of the business right now, and two of these a week is a working studio.

Hand over
$1,500–$3,200

The top of our regular range and a genuinely good one. Hand it over the same day and say so in the note.

Hand over, flagged
$3,200–$8,000

Bigger than one person shoots alone — this is the tier where crew gets hired in. Message Loren directly rather than by email, and do not let it sit overnight.

Call him
$8,000+

Rare, and the reason the ceiling in this brief reaches $20,000. Any mention of an annual budget, a retainer, or a broadcast or national commercial belongs here too. Call Loren the hour you hear it.

08 — What to skip

One number, and a short list

$500 / day

The floor for a day on site, and the only hard rule here. A $500 project is a perfectly normal job when it is one day — what is not a job is anything working out below that rate: a $400 car shoot, forty walkarounds at $150 each, three days of coverage for $900. Those are not small jobs, they are losses. Everything under the floor is a soft steer rather than a ban: if one turns out to have real budget behind it, bring it anyway.

×Weddings and real estate. Fine money, wrong studio — it changes how our other clients price us.
×Volume car dealerships. They want forty inventory walkarounds at $150 each. Exotic and classic dealers are a completely different business, and those we want.
×Equity, revenue share or “exposure.” Decline in one line. No counter-offer needed.
×Free tests and spec work. “Shoot one and if we like it we’ll do twelve” is a free shoot with a story attached.
×Crypto and web3 promos. Payment risk, and it does not sit well on the reel.
×Influencer personal-brand content. Expects production value at creator prices, and often pays in tagged posts.
×Agency subcontracting. Fine occasionally, but they own the client and take most of the money — never let it become the main source.
×Out of region with no travel budget. San Francisco and San Jose are free; everywhere else has to be paid for, and they need to know that early.

The company names are research leads, not vetted prospects — check each still exists and has not just hired in-house. The ones marked with a check are existing clients: cite them as proof and use them as a way in, but do not pitch them.

The budget ranges are what Lumen Haul can realistically close today, with the reel we have — not what the work is worth in the abstract, and not a price list.
